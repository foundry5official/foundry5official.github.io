Four Things People Believe About Custom Software in London

The assumptions UK teams make about bespoke builds, and what actually happens when the work starts.

28 October 2026

There is a story teams tell themselves when the SaaS tool stops fitting: we will just build our own. It sounds decisive. It also hides a set of assumptions that rarely survive contact with a real project. Here are four of the most common, and why each one tends to be wrong.

Myth: Custom software is only for companies with a huge budget

The belief is that bespoke means big consultancy, big invoice, big timeline. In practice the cost question is a comparison, not an absolute. A SaaS product that almost fits carries its own bill: subscription tiers that climb with user count, integration work to bridge the gaps, and the ongoing labour of people doing manually what the tool cannot. None of that shows up as a line item called "software", which is why it gets ignored.

The honest version is that custom software is worth it when your operational logic is the thing that makes you money. If you are running a fairly standard process, an existing tool will usually beat a build on cost and speed. That distinction matters more than budget size.

Myth: You can describe what you want and get it back

A specification document feels like a contract. It is really a starting point. The useful decisions in a build tend to emerge once people see something working and realise the edge case they forgot to mention, or the workflow that reads fine on paper but is awkward in practice.

This is where projects go wrong quietly. A team treats requirements as fixed, the build follows the document faithfully, and the result is technically correct and operationally wrong. A good outcome looks different: the people who do the work day to day stay involved, and the scope is allowed to sharpen as understanding improves. That is not scope creep if it is managed.

Myth: Once it launches, the hard part is over

Go-live is the moment the system meets real data, real users and real load for the first time. Problems surface then that no test environment reproduces. The teams that struggle are the ones who treat launch as the finish line and have nobody on hand when the first odd behaviour appears.

What a good result looks like is unglamorous: monitoring that actually gets watched, a clear route for reporting issues, and a supplier who is still engaged weeks after handover rather than sending an invoice and disappearing.

Myth: Owning the code means you are on your own

Full ownership of source code is worth insisting on, but people sometimes read it as "and now it is your problem". Ownership is about optionality. It means you can bring in another developer, change direction, or sell the business without asking permission.

That optionality only exists if the code is legible to someone who was not there when it was written. Clean history, documentation of why decisions were made, and tests that still mean something a year later are what make ownership real rather than theoretical. Without them you own files, not a system.

What separates a good build from an expensive lesson

The pattern across all four myths is the same. Teams overestimate how much can be settled up front and underestimate how much depends on who is still around afterwards. A good build is not the one with the longest feature list; it is the one where the architecture fits how the business actually runs, the handover is complete enough to be inherited, and the supplier stays accountable once the launch noise dies down.
