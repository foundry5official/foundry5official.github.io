A Kids' App Should Be Harder to Use Than It Looks

Why the instinct to simplify children's software often removes the very thing that makes it worth using.

31 October 2026

The usual advice for anything aimed at children is to strip it back. Fewer taps, bigger buttons, one obvious path from open to close. It sounds sensible, and it is mostly wrong. A children's app that asks nothing of the child is a cartoon with a menu bar, and children abandon those quickly because there is nothing in them to push against. The interesting design problem is not how to remove friction but which friction is doing useful work.

Consider what actually holds a child's attention in a story. It is not the animation, however good it is. It is the sense that something is at stake and that they had a hand in it. That means choices with consequences, moments where the child has to decide what a character should do, and outcomes that differ because of what they picked. Every one of those is, technically, a complication. A single linear path is easier to build, easier to test and easier to ship. It is also the version children put down.

The pull toward a single path

Teams drift toward simplification for understandable reasons. Branching narratives multiply the content you have to write, animate, voice and maintain, and the cost grows faster than the number of branches suggests. Testing explodes because every route needs checking. Parents want controls, so you add a settings layer, and now the interface has two audiences pulling in opposite directions. Faced with that, the temptation is to cut the branching to a token choice or two and call it interactive.

The result usually shows. Children notice when a choice does not matter, and once they notice, the spell breaks. A decision that leads to the same place regardless is worse than no decision at all, because it teaches the child that their input is decorative. That is a strange lesson to build into an app about emotional development.

What good actually looks like

A well-made children's storytelling app feels calm rather than busy. The child can tell what they are choosing and why it matters, without a wall of text explaining it. Characters react in ways that read as emotional rather than mechanical, which means the animation and the writing have to be designed together rather than bolted onto each other. Sound carries a lot of this weight and is routinely underinvested in. And the parental layer has to sit alongside the child's experience without leaking into it, so a six-year-old never meets a screen built for an adult.

None of that is achieved by making the product simpler. It is achieved by deciding, early and deliberately, where the complexity belongs. Some of it belongs in the content pipeline, some in how stories are structured behind the scenes, and some in the testing, where you watch real children use the thing and find out which of your clever choices they ignore. Playtesting with children is humbling in a way that testing with adults is not, because children have no interest in being polite about your interface.

The argument, then, is this: respect the child enough to give them a story where their choices cost something. That is harder to build and harder to test, and it is the difference between an app that gets opened twice and one that becomes part of a routine.
